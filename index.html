Definitions, method and limitations

Definitions

  • eFG% = (FGM + 0.5 × 3PM) ÷ FGA.
  • Defensive / offensive rating = points allowed / scored per 100 possessions. Possessions = FGA − OREB + TO + 0.44 × FTA.
  • Opponent offensive rebound % = share of their own misses opponents grabbed.
  • Factor ranking = (avg in wins − avg in losses) ÷ the standard deviation across games. "Major" is one or more, "Moderate" is at least half.

Limitations

  • Opponent strength isn't weighted. The WIAC-only view is the check for schedule effects.
  • Small sample: 25 games, 14 in conference, only 3 WIAC wins.
  • Correlation isn't causation. Stronger opponents may drive both poor defense and losses.
  • Defense and rebounding overlap: when opponents shoot well there are fewer misses to rebound.

Not factors

  • Pace, three-point attempts, steals and fouls showed little difference.
  • Free-throw shooting stayed around 65–67% in wins and losses: a season-long weakness, not a win/loss driver.

Source

  • Official box scores and play-by-play, all 25 games of 2025–26, compiled into the Blugolds game log workbook by Jasir.

02The question and the data

As a UWEC student and fan, I watched the Blugolds open 11–0 and expected a real run. Instead they went 3–11 over their last 14 games. The question was simple: what actually separated wins from losses, and what could the team do about it?

I built a game-level dataset from the official box scores and play-by-play for all 25 games: shooting, the Four Factors (eFG%, turnover rate, offensive rebounding, free-throw rate), rebounding, scoring breakdown, game flow and player box scores. Every table is keyed on the game number, so every stat links back to one results table.

To judge how much each stat mattered, I compared its average in wins and losses and measured the gap against how much that stat normally varies from game to game (its standard deviation, or SD). A gap of 1 SD or more is labelled major, and half an SD or more moderate. Each stat was then re-checked on WIAC games only. A factor that still holds against conference opponents is a real factor rather than a schedule effect.

Because 25 games is a small sample, each gap also gets a p-value from a permutation test: shuffle the win and loss labels 5,000 times and count how often a gap that big appears by chance. Below 0.05 is the usual bar for "statistically significant". Season-wide, the main gaps clear it easily. Inside the WIAC, with only 3 wins to compare, most don't. Testing about 18 stats at once also raises the odds of a lucky result, so each p-value is adjusted with the Benjamini–Hochberg method into a q-value. After that adjustment, no WIAC-only gap is statistically significant, so conference-only findings are treated as strong hints rather than proof.

Finally, a driver model regresses each game's net rating on the Four Factors and splits the gap between the average win and the average loss into each factor's share. It ties the separate stats into one accounting of where the swing came from.

Data checks: player rows sum exactly to each official team total, and every basket and free throw in the play-by-play adds up to the official final score.

10What didn't matter

11Limitations

  • Opponent strength isn't weighted. Season averages treat every opponent the same. The WIAC-only check is the correction, but it isn't a full strength adjustment.
  • Small sample. 25 games, 14 in conference and only 3 WIAC wins, so a single game can move an average. Most WIAC-only gaps aren't statistically significant for that reason.
  • Correlation, not causation. The four drivers happen together, and the order in this report is an interpretation. Stronger opponents could drive both poor defense and losses.
  • Overlapping factors. Defense, rebounding and halftime margin are linked, so their gaps aren't independent pieces of evidence.

12Recommendations

  1. Make defense the priority, especially protecting the rim and forcing turnovers. It is the biggest lever in the data. Driver 1
  2. Finish possessions with the rebound, so opponents' misses don't become second chances. This is the factor that held up best in conference play. Driver 2
  3. Win the first half. Early deficits almost always held. Driver 3
  4. Keep attacking the rim when behind, instead of drifting into more threes. Driver 4
  5. Plan for the rematch. Second meetings went worse, so they deserve their own adjustments. When and where
  6. Find out why Xavier Lewis faded in losses. He played the same minutes but produced about a third less per minute, so the question is how he was used, not how long. Players

13Next steps

  • Watch a practice. The data shows where the problem is. Observation would show why: how defense and rebounding are taught and drilled.
  • Adjust for opponent strength. Add opponent ratings so every stat accounts for the quality of competition.
  • Add lineups and shot locations. See which combinations defend and rebound best, and where opponents' good looks came from.
  • Test the second-half slide. Check minutes and rotations to see whether fatigue or substitutions explain the lost second halves.
